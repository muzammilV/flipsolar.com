Solar Panels for Restaurants in Texas:
The Complete 2026 Guide

☀️ FlipSolar Editorial Team 📅 Updated October 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read 🤠 Texas

If you own or operate a restaurant in Texas, electricity is almost certainly one of your top three operating costs. Between commercial HVAC, kitchen equipment, refrigeration, and lighting, a mid-sized Texas restaurant burns through $2,000–$5,000 in electricity every single month.

Solar can cut that bill significantly. And the 30% federal tax credit is still available — but under current law only for systems placed in service by December 31, 2027 (unless construction began by July 4, 2026) — so timing matters for Texas restaurant owners.

This guide covers everything — illustrative cost numbers, realistic savings, the tax credit, payback timelines, and how to avoid the installers who will waste your time.

💡 Bottom line upfront: A Texas restaurant paying $3,000/month can offset a large share of that bill with solar. On bill savings plus the 30% ITC alone, simple payback typically works out to roughly 8–12 years — and can be shorter with depreciation, financing and higher local rates. Pricing and savings vary by site, so compare quotes.

Why Texas Restaurants Are Ideal Solar Candidates

Texas was the #1 state in the country for new solar capacity added in 2025, according to SEIA and Wood Mackenzie. There are three reasons restaurants specifically are such strong solar candidates:

  • High daytime energy consumption. Restaurants run heavy loads during daylight hours — exactly when solar produces the most power. Your lunch and dinner prep, HVAC and refrigeration all run while the sun is up.
  • Large roof or parking area. Most Texas restaurant buildings have flat commercial roofs or adjacent land ideal for a ground-mounted array.
  • Rising electricity rates. Commercial electricity costs can climb over time. Solar fixes the cost of the portion of your usage it covers.

How Much Does Solar Cost for a Texas Restaurant?

System cost depends on how much of your bill you want to offset — and how much roof or land you have. These figures are illustrative. They assume about 10¢/kWh, roughly 1,500 kWh produced per kW per year, an installed cost of $1.80–$2.55 per watt, and a system sized to offset about 70% of the bill.

  • Small café / QSR (bill: $1,500/mo): ~85 kW · Before incentives: $150,000–$215,000 · After 30% ITC: $105,000–$150,000
  • Mid-size restaurant (bill: $3,000/mo): ~170 kW · Before incentives: $300,000–$430,000 · After 30% ITC: $210,000–$300,000
  • Large restaurant (bill: $6,000/mo): ~335 kW · Before incentives: $605,000–$855,000 · After 30% ITC: $425,000–$600,000

📍 Roof space matters: A system this size needs roughly 60 sq ft per kW (about 10,000 sq ft for 170 kW). Many restaurants can only host a smaller system, which costs proportionally less and offsets a smaller share of the bill.

📌 Important: These are pre-financing numbers. Many Texas restaurants finance solar with little or no money down through a solar loan; depending on the terms, the monthly payment may be lower than the bill it replaces.

The 30% Federal Investment Tax Credit (ITC)

  • Your business may claim a 30% tax credit on the installed cost of a qualifying system — a credit against tax owed, not a deduction.
  • On a $100,000 system, that is up to $30,000 off your federal tax bill. Because it reduces taxes you owe, you need tax liability to use it.
  • Timing matters. Under current law (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025), a system that did not begin construction by July 4, 2026 must be placed in service by December 31, 2027 to qualify. Projects that began construction by July 4, 2026 have longer to finish.
  • Depreciation rules for solar, including bonus depreciation, have also changed recently. Ask your tax advisor what applies to you.
  • This is general information, not tax advice.

An Illustrative Example

Assumptions: about 10¢/kWh, about 1,500 kWh produced per kW per year, $1.80–$2.55 per watt installed, the 30% ITC applied, and no depreciation or financing effects.

  • Monthly electricity bill before solar: $3,000 (about 30,000 kWh).
  • Roof-limited system, 60 kW: costs about $108,000–$153,000 before incentives ($76,000–$107,000 after the ITC), produces about 90,000 kWh a year and saves roughly $9,000 a year (about 25% of the bill) — a simple payback of roughly 8–12 years.
  • Large-roof system, 170 kW: costs about $300,000–$430,000 before incentives ($210,000–$300,000 after the ITC), offsets about 70% of the bill and saves roughly $25,000 a year — also roughly 8–12 years.
  • Faster paybacks are possible with higher electricity rates, depreciation, lower installed costs or favorable financing; slower paybacks are possible if your system costs more or your usage is lower. Your quotes will tell you.

Solar Financing Options for Texas Restaurants

  1. Cash purchase. Best long-term ROI. You own the system outright and claim the full ITC. Payback depends on your rates, system cost and tax situation — then 20+ years of low-cost power.
  2. Solar loan (low or no money down). A common option. Depending on the terms, the monthly payment may be close to or below your current electricity bill, so you can start saving with little or no capital outlay.
  3. Solar lease / PPA. Fixed monthly rate for power generated. No ITC benefit but no maintenance responsibility. Good for restaurants with thin capital.
  4. PACE financing. Property Assessed Clean Energy — attached to your property taxes, not your credit. Available in many Texas counties.

  • Are you NABCEP certified?
  • Who handles the permit and utility interconnection paperwork?

  • Do you handle the ITC documentation and incentive filings?

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does installation take?

Most commercial installations in Texas take 2–4 weeks from permit approval to final inspection. Total timeline from quote to live system: 2–4 months.

Will solar panels affect my restaurant's operations?

Minimal disruption. Good installers schedule rooftop work during off-hours. The grid connection switch-over typically takes just 2–4 hours.

What happens on cloudy days or at night?

Your restaurant stays connected to the ERCOT grid and draws power normally. You only pay for what solar doesn't cover. Many restaurants add a battery backup to reduce reliance on peak-rate grid power.

Can I get solar if I lease my restaurant building?

It is possible but requires landlord cooperation. The best approach is a Solar Rider Agreement attached to your lease. Get 3 quotes first so you know the numbers before approaching your landlord.
